Open when your lecturer says

Meaning changed. The BBC says employers added 206,000 jobs. Advertised means offered vacancies, which is a different thing: a job can be advertised and never filled. "Over 205,000" is also less precise than 206,000.

Turnitin would not notice: the words are different, so the similarity score is low. Only a reader who checks the source sees the error. Check every verb and every number against the original.

Although AI tools can support learning, several potential disadvantages of their use in education have been identified. Both sources warn that heavy reliance on AI may reduce human contact: AI cannot replace the emotional support that teachers provide (Shonubi, 2023), and students who interact more with software than with teachers may feel isolated, lose motivation and be more likely to drop out (Walden University, 2024). A second concern is the quality of the information AI produces. Because these systems depend on their training data, they may give biased answers that reinforce stereotypes, assign unfair grades, or spread inaccurate or outdated information (Walden University, 2024). Academic integrity is a further issue, since students can use tools such as ChatGPT to complete their work, while the programs designed to detect this may wrongly flag original writing (Walden University, 2024). In addition, the cost of developing and introducing AI tools may be a barrier for schools with limited resources, and these tools may collect and store sensitive personal data, which raises privacy and security concerns (Shonubi, 2023). However, the fear that AI will replace teachers appears less justified, as it is intended to assist them rather than make them redundant (Shonubi, 2023).

Notice: shared theme first with both sources cited; every borrowed point has its own citation; linking words carry the argument; the counter-point closes the paragraph.

SWAPS · five levers for a real paraphrase

Part 1
SSynonymscheaper > less expensive
WWord formsubsidise > a subsidy
AActive / passive80% use the BRT > the BRT is used by 80%
PPosition of ideasstart with the result, then the cause
SStatistics & time80% > four out of five; a day > daily

Green flags

  • Three or more levers used
  • Every verb and number checked against the original
  • Names, terms and precise numbers kept
  • Citation next to the borrowed idea
  • Written from notes with the original hidden

Red flags

  • Only synonyms swapped (patchwriting)
  • Copied wording with a citation but no quotation marks
  • A number or verb that says more or less than the source
  • No citation because "the words are mine"
  • Paraphrasing tools or AI-written text

3-check: Meaning kept? Look changed? Source cited?

4W reference check · Who, When, What, Where

Part 2APA 7
WWHO · AuthorSurname, Initials. All authors up to 20, comma before &. Or the organisation.
WWHEN · Date(Year). News and web: (Year, Month Day). Same author and year: 2011a, 2011b.
WWHAT · TitleSentence case. Italics for books and stand-alone web pages.
WWHERE · SourceJournal, volume(issue), pages, DOI. Or publisher. Or site and URL.

Green flags

  • Every citation matches one entry: same spelling, same year
  • Built from the source itself
  • A to Z by first author's surname, hanging indent
  • DOI written as https://doi.org/10....

Red flags

  • "et al." on the reference list
  • A DOI that does not start with 10.
  • Copied straight from an app or database without checking
  • Listing a source you did not read
  • "Retrieved from", place of publication, [Accessed ...] (older styles)

Match rule: every in-text citation has one entry, and every entry has at least one citation.

Quote, paraphrase or summarise?

Quote

  • Exact definition, memorable wording, or you respond to the exact words
  • Under 40 words: "double quotation marks" + page: (Ervin et al., 2018, p. 470)
  • 40+ words: block quote, indented 0.5 in, no quotation marks, citation after the final full stop

Paraphrase or summarise

  • Your normal choice
  • Paraphrase: same ideas and detail, your words and structure
  • Summary: selected main points, shorter
  • Both always cited

Sentence frames for Assessment 1

  • According to [Author] ([Year]), ...
  • [Author] ([Year]) reports / argues / suggests / shows that ...
  • ... ([Author], [Year]).
  • [Author] ([Year]) outlines three ...: ..., ... and ....
  • Both [A] ([Year]) and [B] ([Year]) highlight ... / ... ([A], [Year]; [B], [Year]).
  • While [A] ([Year]) argues that ..., [B] ([Year]) suggests ...
  • [Author] ([Year]) defines ... as "..." (p. [n]).
  • ... ([Original], [Year], as cited in [Author], [Year]).
  • As Figure 1 shows, ... Below the figure: Note. Data from [Author] ([Year]).

Choose the verb that matches the source's certainty: suggests is tentative, shows is strong. Do not upgrade "may" to "always".
