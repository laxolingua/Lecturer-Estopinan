Before you read on: a museum visit is not "research" unless you record it like a researcher. The Futurium is a primary source — when you note what an exhibit claims, who made it, and what evidence it shows, you can cite it in APA exactly like a journal article. This sheet is how you do that.
1
Before you walk in · 10 minutes

Lock your focus

You can't observe everything. Decide what you're hunting for, so the visit stays tied to your report instead of becoming a general day out.

1.1In one sentence, what is your Assessment 1 case study about?

A specific company, industry aspect, or country example within sustainability — not "the environment" in general.

1.2Write the working thesis you will defend.

A claim you can argue, not a topic. Pattern: "[Case] is / is not sustainable because…, although…"

1.3Three things you will actively look for today.

Tie each to your case: a statistic, a competing viewpoint, a real-world example, a counterargument, a future scenario.

The Futurium has three core areas — Nature, Human, and Technology (the Futurium "Worlds"), plus the hands-on Futurium Lab in the basement. The zones below map to these. Don't try to do all of them deeply — go where your case study lives.

Build your own data table

Assessment 1 requires one self-created graph or table from Excel. Collect the raw numbers here today, then build the chart at home. Find any exhibit that shows quantities over time, or a comparison between options — energy mixes, emissions by sector, transport modes, costs, years.

What does this data measure? (your table title)
Where in the Futurium did you find it? (for your APA citation)
Category / Year / LabelValue (with unit)
Tonight: drop these into Excel, make a column or line chart, give it a numbered caption ("Figure 1. …"), and refer to it in your body text ("As Figure 1 shows…"). That single move satisfies a required element and lifts your Formatting score.
Teacher noteIf an exhibit gives a graphic but no exact numbers, tell students to estimate values from the visual and label the table "approximate values read from exhibit display." That is honest and still citable. The skill being practised is data extraction, not precision to the decimal.

Don't wait until tonight. While it's fresh, draft one real body paragraph using the structure you practised in Weeks 5–6: Claim → Evidence → Warrant → Objection → Refutation.

Teacher reference · models, not marks

Worked exemplars & expected responses

There are no fixed "correct" answers to a field worksheet, but students need to see what a strong response looks like. Use these as models to show on a phone/tablet or to recognise quality while circulating.

Model: a strong evidence card (Section 2)

Exhibit: Interactive display on urban food production / vertical farming (Nature World).

Claim (student's words): The exhibit argues that growing food inside cities could cut the emissions caused by transporting produce long distances.

Evidence: It shows that food can travel thousands of kilometres before reaching a plate, and presents vertical farms using a fraction of the water of field agriculture.

Use: Supports my thesis that localised food systems improve sustainability — but I must check the energy cost of indoor lighting, which the exhibit downplays.

Why it's strong: it extracts a claim and evidence (not just "there was a farming wall"), and it already spots a limitation. That last move is what earns Content marks.

Model: the counterargument box (Section 2.x)

"One exhibit suggested that electric mobility still depends on mining for batteries, which carries its own environmental and human cost. This complicates my thesis that EVs are clearly sustainable, because it shifts the harm rather than removing it. I will respond in my report by arguing that sustainability should be judged across the whole supply chain, not just at the point of use."

Why it's strong: names a real trade-off, states how it threatens the thesis, and gives a refutation strategy — the full Objection–Refutation move.

Model: body paragraph (Section 4.1)

Claim. Bringing food production into cities offers a measurable sustainability gain. Evidence. An exhibit at the Futurium (2026) demonstrated that conventional produce can travel long distances to market, while indoor systems use far less water. Warrant. Shorter supply chains reduce transport emissions, a core driver of the food system's footprint. Objection. However, indoor farms rely on artificial lighting, which can raise energy demand. Refutation. Where that energy is drawn from renewable sources, the net effect remains positive, suggesting the model is sustainable only when paired with clean power.

Why it's strong: every move is present and ordered; the source is cited in-text; the conclusion is conditional rather than absolute, which reads as "sophisticated reasoning."

APA guidance for citing the museum

In-text: (Futurium, 2026).

Reference list pattern for an exhibition: Futurium. (2026). Title of the exhibit or permanent exhibition [Exhibition]. Futurium, Berlin.

Remind students the museum counts as one source and is primary, not academic — it does not satisfy the "2 journal articles" requirement. It works best as illustration alongside peer-reviewed sources, not as the backbone of the argument.

Open flag for you: course materials mix APA 6 and APA 7. Confirm which edition you want them citing before they submit this week, and state it once on the board.

What "done well" looks like overall

A successful sheet leaves the building with: 3+ evidence cards that extract claim + evidence, one genuine counterargument, 4+ rows of real numbers for a chart, one drafted C-E-W-O-R paragraph, and an APA reference. A weak sheet has descriptions of exhibits with no argument and no numbers. The fastest diagnostic while circulating: point at any card and ask "what does it argue?" If they restate what they saw, redirect.
